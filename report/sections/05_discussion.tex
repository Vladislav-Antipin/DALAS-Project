\section{Discussion}

The best-tuned model, XGBoost, showed moderate performance on the test data, with slightly higher
precision (0.658) than recall (0.595). This indicates that the model produces more false negatives
 than false positives on this dataset. Since the primary goal is to prioritize new drug--disease indications,
higher precision  ensures that predictions labeled as positive are more reliable, while lower recall 
implies that some potentially promising combinations may be (systematically and unfairly) overlooked. 
Other models performed slightly worse, particularly kNN, suggesting that they struggled to capture
 non-linear patterns in noisy high-dimensional data or to handle class imbalance. 

Heterogenous GNN was trained and tested separately and produced a large number
of false positives, resulting in low precision of 0.429. This poor performance may be partially due to
poor handling of class imbalance on our side, as we used a binary cross-entropy loss with positive weights 
proportional to the relative rarity of success labels, which may have been too stringent for the model. 
Further investigation is needed to identify proper strategies for handling class imbalance, 
as well as for hyperparameter and classification threshold tuning in GNNs. Other potential explanations for a poor model's performance include
the limited size of the training dataset, which may be insufficient for training such a complex model,
 as well as the need for strategies to prevent overfitting.

The two most important features identified by permutation importance are pathway similarity and number of indications for a disease,
with higher values of both increasing the predicted probability of success. Pathway similarity is rather expected to
be predictive, as it captures shared molecular mechanisms underlying drug action and disease pathogenesis.
It is also unfortunately expected to see the number of indications being predictive of success since this feature reflects
how intensively researched the disease is, this constitutes a bias towards well-studied diseases and potentially neglecting 
rare or poorly studied ones. However, analysis of feature distributions for correctly and incorrectly classified cases didn't reveal 
any pronounced bias. This suggests that importance of number of indications likely reflects a bias in clinical research itself rather than a bias in the model.
It can be also a reflection of data availability bias - rare diseases tend to be less annotated.

Another important source of bias is label assignment. Assessing the success of clinical trials is challenging due to poorly published 
results, and many drug--disease combinations have never been tested. 
Some combinations were not pursued because of clear
 mechanistic incompatibility, while others were limited by funding constraints.
 Even when a drug--disease indication is shown to be ineffective in a trial, it was typically preselected 
 from thousands of potential alternatives as promising, otherwise, it would not have received funding.
  Furthermore, there is a publication bias reflected in
 better publication quality and documentation of successful trials, whereas some failed 
 trials may be prematurely terminated without clear justification (e.g. citing funding issues while
 concealing drug inefficiency).
 One potential solution is to include "obvious negatives" combinations in the dataset or to
  employ semisupervised approaches, such as Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning models, 
 which rely on the positive class and treat the remaining data data as unlabeled (mixture of positive and negative cases),
  allowing for indirect reconstruction of the negative class.
