We then used XGBoost for final evaluation on test, achieving a precision of 0.658, recall of 0.595 and F1 score of 0.625 (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-confusion_matrix}). 

The precision-recall curve (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-pr}) shows a rather smooth decline from 1 to chance level
 (success prevalence of 0.313),  indicating that precision does not drop abruptly as the model becomes less strict (higher recall) 
 and that overall performance is relatively stable. 
 The area under the ROC curve is 0.824 (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-roc}), however, 
this metric is less reliable in the presence of class imbalance.

We also trained a Heterogenous GNN, achieving a precision of 0.429, 
recall of 0.714 and F1 score 0.535 (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-gnn-confusion_matrix}). Unlike the other machine learning
models, the GNN was evaluated independently for two reasons. First, GNN implementation in this project is rather
exploratory, and we cannot yet ensure that all learning parameters were correctly
configured. Second, differences in model architecture make direct comparison with other models
in the standard cross-validation setting non-trivial. Therefore, GNN results should be interpreted 
as preliminary and complementary.



\subsection{Feature Analysis}

Since XGBoost model is not interpretable by design, we applied post-hoc explanation
methods. We used permutation feature importance (evaluated using the F1 score) and partial dependence analysis
from \verb|sklearn| module (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-perm_importance}). Permuting the pathway similarity feature led to a
 15\% decrease in F1 score, while the number of indications for a disease (proxy of disease "popularity") and 6-th component in disease pathway embedding space
 caused decreases of approximately 3.5\%. Other features, including various disease and drug pathway embeddings and molecular fingerprints, resulted in less than 3.5\% decrease in F1 score.

The one-dimensional partial dependence plot (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-1d_partial_dependence}) indicates that the 
steepest increase in success prediction occurs at a pathway similarity of around 0.25 and approximately 70 indications per disease. 
The two-dimensional partial dependence plot for pathway similarity and number of indications (Figure~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-2d_partial_dependence})
shows that the model requires both high pathway similarity and a large number of indications to confidently predict a successful outcome.

\subsection{Failure and Success Cases}

We added features to the test set indicating whether each observation is true positive (TP),
true negative (TN), false positive (FP) or false negative (FN) and simply correctly classified (TP or TN).
Pearson correlations between these confusion indicators and predictor features were computed, 
with caution: since the new variables are binary, these correlations should be interpreted as point-biserial
 correlations  (Figures~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-corr_matrix-confusion}~and~\ref{fig:04-time_split-gnn-corr_matrix-confusion}). 
We also examined the distributions of predictors for correctly versus incorrectly classified 
cases (Figures~\ref{fig:04-time_split-xgb-feature_distribution_by_correct}~and~\ref{fig:04-time_split-gnn-feature_distribution_by_correct}). 
Overall there are no major differences, and while some variables show slight biases, their interpretation remains speculative.
